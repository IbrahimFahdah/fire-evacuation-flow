How the simulation works

Each person is an agent that knows the shortest way out and pushes through a crowd according to the Social Force Model. Queues, arching at doors and bottlenecks come out of those two rules; none of them are scripted.

1 · From drawing to walkable floor

The DXF is read layer by layer. You assign each layer a role (wall, door, fire door, stair, exit signage, furniture, fixed obstacle, ignore). Walls, columns, lift shafts and, optionally, desks become obstacles on a 0.1 m grid. Doors are read from their blocks: the swing arc gives the hinge, the width and the side the leaf opens to. Moving a door patches the original opening shut and cuts a new one.

Exits are suggested, never assumed. A door scores points for being a fire door, for an EXIT sign or “STAIR / ESCAPE / مخرج” text nearby, and above all for opening into an enclosure that contains stair geometry, or onto the outside. You confirm the list.

2 · Route choice — distance field

For every exit the app computes the walking distance from every point on the floor (Dijkstra on a 16-neighbour grid, close to the continuous eikonal solution used in Hughes’ crowd model and in floor-field models). Paths are kept 0.2 m clear of obstacles, so nobody is routed through a gap a body cannot pass, and pay a small penalty for hugging walls. Each person follows the downhill direction of their exit’s field. In “avoid long queues” mode a person re-weighs each exit every second as walking time plus queue length ÷ door capacity.

Two practical additions keep small groups from locking up in a way real people don’t: someone who has not moved for a few seconds turns their shoulders (body radius shrinks up to 20%) and squeezes past a jamb, and the random fluctuation grows while they are stuck. Neither affects flow in a moving queue, which the Calibration tab checks.

4 · Pre-movement

Total time = detection and alarm delay + each person’s response time + walking + queuing. The PD 7974-6 scenarios give the response time of the first occupants (1st percentile) and the last (99th percentile); the app fits a log-normal distribution through those two points, which matches the long tail seen in evacuation drills. You can also enter your own distribution. Because response time dominates many results, use the Monte Carlo runs to see the spread.

5 · What the heatmap shows

Local density is a Gaussian-weighted head count within about 1 m of each moving person. Density paints the highest density each spot reached, banded by Fruin’s Level of Service for walkways (A < 0.31, B < 0.43, C < 0.72, D < 1.08, E < 2.17, F ≥ 2.17 persons/m²). Queue time paints how many seconds each spot spent above 1.08 p/m² (LOS E–F), which is where the bottlenecks are.

6 · The hand calculation alongside

When a run ends, the Results card also shows the SFPE hydraulic estimate: alarm + 99th-percentile response + the longer of the walk to each exit at 1.19 m/s or the time for that exit’s share of people to pass its door at 1.32 persons/s per metre of effective width (clear width minus 0.15 m each side). Agreement between the two is a sanity check; differences point at geometry the hand method can’t see, such as a crowd merging at a corridor door.

7 · Code checks (UAE Fire & Life Safety Code, 2018)

Pick the occupancy and the sprinkler status; the limits come from Chapter 3. Travel distance is the longest shortest walk from any occupied point to an exit, measured along the route with the same wall clearance the simulation uses (the code asks for 305 mm round corners). Common path is the distance walked before the routes to two different exits split: for exits A and B it is (walk to A + walk to B − walk from A to B) ÷ 2, which is exact in corridor networks. The Common path map colours every point by that distance, so dead-end rooms and corridors stand out in red where they exceed the limit. Remoteness uses ⅓ of the floor diagonal when sprinklered (½ when not), measured as walking distance between exits for low- and mid-rise buildings and as a straight line for high-rise (Table 3.15.a). Capacity uses 7.6 mm per person on stairs and 5 mm on doors and level routes (Table 3.13); losing any one exit must leave at least half the required capacity (§4.2.2).

Limits — read before quoting a number

  • Single floor. Exits are the protected stair doors (or doors to outside); the optional stair-discharge limit approximates the stair below, but floors don’t merge.
  • No smoke, no fire growth, no ASET. Compare the clearance time with an ASET from your fire engineer.
  • No groups, families, staff wardens or people with reduced mobility.
  • Code checks are prescriptive and use the UAE Fire & Life Safety Code (September 2018) tables. Dead ends, single-exit permissions and occupancy-specific rules are not assessed. They are not a substitute for review by Civil Defence or an accredited consultant.
  • Not a validated egress tool like Pathfinder or MassMotion. Use it to explore and explain options, not to sign them off.
